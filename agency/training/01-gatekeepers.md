# Module 01: Getting Past the Gatekeeper (Honestly)

**Who this is for:** Vanguard setters cold calling US local service businesses.
**What you'll be able to do:** get the owner on the phone, or get a real callback time, without lying, and without burning the person who answered.
**Use with:** `agency/SETTER_SCRIPT.md` (the owner conversation) and `agency/industries/*.md` (niche timing and language).

> Setter reality check: "High chances are someone from the business owner's team picks up the call and will throw smokescreens to avoid providing the number or putting you through. Receptionists are the #1 barrier."

This module is about that first 20 seconds, before you ever reach the owner.

---

## The ethics rule (read this first)

Vanguard does not use deception. Not to get past a gatekeeper, and not ever. That means you never:

- Pretend to be a customer, a patient, a vendor they already use, or "a friend."
- Claim a prior relationship ("We spoke last week," "I'm returning his call") when there wasn't one.
- Imply the owner asked for the call or is expecting it.
- Say it's "personal," "about his account," "about an invoice," or "urgent."
- Say you're "calling about your Google listing" in a way that sounds like you're from Google. We are not Google. Scammers use that line, so receptionists are trained to hang up on it.
- Hide or dodge who you are or what company you're with.
- Use a spouse, kid, or employee's name as a pretext.

If someone asks a direct question, give a direct, true answer. The good news is that we don't need tricks. Our honest reason ("we already built you a free demo website") is more interesting than most lies.

Throughout this module, if a well-known gatekeeper tactic depends on deception, you'll see it flagged as **Excluded** with the honest version we use instead.

## 1. Why gatekeepers block, and what they're listening for

### Who the "gatekeeper" actually is at a local business

At small local businesses there's rarely a trained receptionist. It's usually:

| Business | Who usually answers |
|---|---|
| Salon / barbershop / nail / spa | Front desk person, or a stylist or barber between cuts, sometimes with a client in the chair |
| Trades (roofing, HVAC, plumbing, contractors) | Owner's spouse, an office manager who handles the books, or an answering service. Owner is on a roof or under a sink |
| Auto repair / body shop | Service writer at the counter. Owner is in the bays |
| Towing | Dispatcher, often the owner themselves |
| Gyms / martial arts | Front desk during peak hours, a coach, or the owner |
| Restaurants | Host, server, or manager in the middle of a rush |
| Pet groomers / cleaners / daycares | Groomer with a dog on the table, a cleaner on a job, a daycare teacher |

Two things follow from this:

1. **They're busy doing real work.** You are interrupting a haircut, a phone order, a customer at the counter. Short and polite wins.
2. **They're often an influencer, not just a wall.** In trades especially, the spouse or office manager who answers often handles the books. Our `trades.md` file says to treat them as "a gatekeeper *and* an influencer." Win them and they'll walk the message to the owner themselves.

### What they're protecting

The person who answers has a simple job: don't waste the owner's time. Local businesses get hammered by SEO spam, "Google listing" robocalls, merchant-services reps, and Yelp ad sellers. So in the first few seconds they're listening for one thing: **"Is this a vendor or telemarketer?"**

What makes you sound like one (avoid all of these):

- **Speed.** Receptionists interviewed by UK Recruiter said a caller "speaking at a million miles an hour" usually signals an unsolicited sales call.
- **Fake warmth.** "Hi! How are you doing today?!" 30 Minutes to President's Club (Armand Farrokh and Nick Cegelski) specifically warn against opening with "How's your day going?" because strangers don't care.
- **Vagueness or dodging.** The same receptionists said callers who avoid their questions, give orders, or "conceal their identity" lose any desire to help.
- **Not knowing the owner's name.** It shows you're dialing a list.
- **Scripted, upward-ending tone.** In *Cold Calling Sucks (And That's Why It Works)*, Farrokh and Cegelski call out rushed, robotic, upward-inflected delivery as what makes an opener sound canned.
- **Over-politeness and long explanations.** A 40-second explanation is a pitch, and pitches to the front desk get "send us an email."

What makes them help:

- You know the owner's name.
- You say who you are and why you're calling, briefly and plainly.
- You're calm, unhurried, and polite ("please" and "thank you").
- You respect that they're busy and ask for *help*, not *access*.
- You give them something easy to do: tell you a good time, or pass on a two-line message.

Kevin Dorsey's framing (from a compiled collection of his quotes, Live Better Sell Better podcast) applies here as much as with owners: everyone on a call wants to know two things. **Who are you, and why are you calling me?** Answer both quickly and honestly and most of the suspicion goes away.

## 2. Mindset: they're your ally, not your obstacle

### Treat them like a teammate

Many trainers make the same point: you don't "get around" the gatekeeper, you get *help* from them. MTD Sales Training frames getting past the gatekeeper as a sale in itself. Apollo's guide calls gatekeepers "routing points" whose job is to send calls to the right place. Our job is to give them a clear, credible reason to route us to the owner.

Practical version for a setter:

- **Assume they're smart and on the owner's side.** They are. Act like you're on the owner's side too, because you are (we built something for their business).
- **Never be annoyed.** If you get blocked, be gracious. You'll be calling this number again, and that person remembers you.
- **Get their name and use it later, lightly.** "Thanks, Jess. I'll try him at 7:30 tomorrow." Next call: "Hi, is this Jess? It's Jordan from Vanguard again." Note: don't overdo first names on the first call. Some receptionists find it presumptuous.

### Tone, pace, confidence

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| Speak at a normal, slightly slower pace | Race through it |
| Use downward inflection on requests ("Is Mike around." not "Is Mike around?!") | End every sentence like a question |
| Sound like you're calling a business you have a reason to call (you do) | Sound apologetic ("Sorry to bother you, I know you're busy, I just...") |
| Keep it short: 1 to 2 sentences, then stop talking | Explain the whole offer to the front desk |
| Smile, sound relaxed | Fake enthusiasm |

**Confidence comes from preparation, not attitude.** Before you dial (see SETTER_SCRIPT "Before You Dial"), know the owner's first name, the business name, the one specific gap (no site, no booking, broken on mobile), and have the demo built and tested. When you actually have something built for them, you don't need to bluff.

Jeremy Miner (7th Level, NEPQ) teaches that sounding like a typical salesperson triggers resistance, and pushes a calm, curious, low-pressure tone instead of an eager pitch. That's the tone to use with gatekeepers too: neutral, relaxed, not needy. (We didn't find a public Miner gatekeeper script, so no lines here are attributed to him.)

### The "ledge"

When a gatekeeper pushes back, don't snap back with an answer. Have a short acknowledgment ready that buys you a beat to stay calm. 30MPC calls this "the ledge" and Armand Farrokh credits the idea to Jeb Blount. Examples:

- "Totally fair."
- "Yeah, that makes sense."
- "Good question."

Then give your honest answer.

## 3. Openers to the gatekeeper (word for word)

**Rules for every opener:**
- Say your name **and** "Vanguard" in the first sentence. Never withhold the company. (Receptionists say it only wastes time, and hiding it reads as shady.)
- Ask for the owner by first name if you have it.
- Stop talking after the ask.

### Opener 1: Owner's name known (default)
> "Hi, this is Jordan with Vanguard. Is Mike around?"

Short, casual, sounds like a normal business call. This is our honest version of the 30MPC "slide-by" (a short, first-name ask). The difference: **we always include the company name.** Some versions of the slide-by have you give only your first name so you sound like someone the owner knows. We don't do that, because it relies on a false impression.

### Opener 2: "Please" twice (Jeb Blount)
Reader highlights from *Fanatical Prospecting* show Blount recommending you use "please" twice, e.g. "Would you please connect me to Mike Brooks, please?" Small-business version:
> "Hi, this is Jordan with Vanguard. Could you please let Mike know I'm on the line, please?"

It sounds polite and expectant without claiming anything false.

### Opener 3: Owner's name unknown, the "help me out" ask
> "Hi, I'm hoping you can help me out. My name's Jordan, I'm with Vanguard. Who's the owner there?"
*(They give a name.)*
> "Great, thank you. Is Sarah around by chance?"

Asking for help puts them in the helper role instead of the screener role. If they won't give the name, see Section 5.

### Opener 4: Upfront and honest (the reason in one breath)
Use this when the person sounds sharp or suspicious, or on a second attempt.
> "Hi, this is Jordan with Vanguard, we build websites for local businesses. I'll be straight with you, this is a sales call. We already built a demo website for [Business Name] and I'm trying to get 15 minutes with the owner to show it to him. Is Mike in today?"

Admitting it's a sales call is disarming. 30MPC recommends being "disarmingly blunt" about the fact that it's a cold call instead of hiding it. It also answers Dorsey's two questions (who and why) before they're asked.

### Opener 5: Busy-shop opener (salons, barbers, groomers, auto counters)
> "Hey, sounds busy over there, I'll be quick. Jordan with Vanguard. Is Tasha free between clients at all today?"

Acknowledging the noise shows you get their world. Asking "between clients" invites a time, not a "no."

### Opener 6: The answerer might be the owner
At very small shops the owner often answers. If the listing has a personal name or cell:
> "Hi, is this Mike?"
- If yes: go straight to the SETTER_SCRIPT owner opener.
- If "Who's calling?": "Jordan with Vanguard, we build websites for local businesses. Is this Mike?"

### Opener 7: No-oriented ask (Chris Voss style)
From Chris Voss (*Never Split the Difference*): questions that let people say "no" feel safer to answer. Use it when you're asking for something extra.
> "Would it be a bad idea to ask when Mike's usually off the job sites?"
> "Would you be against me trying him first thing tomorrow?"

### Opener 8: Callback opener (second attempt, honest)
> "Hi, is this Jess? It's Jordan from Vanguard again. You told me to try Mike around 7:30, so here I am. Is he around?"

Only use "you told me" if they actually did. Never invent a previous instruction.

**Excluded:** "Hi, it's Jordan, is Mike there?" (first name only, refusing to give the company when asked), "I'm returning Mike's call," "Mike asked me to call," "It's personal," "I'm a customer of yours."

## 4. The top 10 smokescreens, with word-for-word responses

**General pattern for every pushback:**
1. **Ledge.** ("Totally fair.")
2. **Honest answer in one sentence.** Who you are and why.
3. **Small, easy ask.** "Is he around?" or "When's the best time to catch him?"

If they push back a second time on the same call, don't fight. Move to "get the best time" (Section 5) or leave a message (Section 7). Bailing after two pushbacks is a common rule (it's part of how secondary sources describe 30MPC's "Triple Bypass"), and it keeps the gatekeeper on your side for the next call.

---

### 1. "What's this regarding?"
> "Sure. I'm with Vanguard, we build websites for local businesses. We came across [Business Name] on Google, noticed there's no website, and we already built a free demo of one using your services and info. I just want to get 15 minutes with Mike to show it to him. Is he around?"

Shorter version for a rushed front desk:
> "Good question. We built a free demo website for [Business Name], and I wanted to show it to Mike. Is he in?"

Why it works: it's true, specific, and the demo is curiosity-worthy. Don't give the whole pitch. One sentence of "what" and one of "ask."

### 2. "Is he expecting your call?"
> "No, he's not. This is a cold call, I'll be honest. We built a demo website for [Business Name] and I'm hoping to get a couple of minutes with him. Is now a bad time, or is there a better time to catch him?"

**Excluded:** "Yes," "He should be," or "We've been in touch." Former executive assistants quoted in Apollo's guide say exactly this: "Don't lie. Don't tell them the leader is expecting your call." Lies get remembered, and in a small business, the person who answers *talks to the owner every day*.

### 3. "He's busy / he's with a client / he's on a job."
> "No problem, I figured he might be. When's he usually free, between clients or after he's off the job site?"

Then lock a time:
> "Perfect. I'll try him at 4:15. Should I call this number, or is there a better way to reach him then?"

Industry file note (beauty-wellness.md): if they're with a client, don't pitch. Ask when the next break is and **call back exactly then.**

### 4. "Send us an email."
> "Happy to. What's the best email for Mike directly?"
*(Get it.)*
> "And so I don't keep calling you, when's a good time for me to follow up with him by phone after he's had a look?"

If they give a general info@ inbox:
> "That works. Could you put his name on it for me, so it doesn't get lost? And what's your name, so I can mention you told me to send it there?"

Notes:
- Per `DEMO_PLAYBOOK.md`, **don't send the demo link to the gatekeeper.** Send the owner-facing email from SETTER_SCRIPT ("Demo site for [Business Name]") addressed to the owner by name.
- Apollo's guide suggests getting the gatekeeper's name and offering to copy them. That's fine, as long as the email is addressed to the owner.
- Always get a follow-up phone time. "Send an email" with no follow-up time is usually a polite no.

### 5. "We're not interested."
> "Totally fair, and I'm not asking you to be. I'm just trying to show the owner a demo we already built for the shop. Is website stuff something Mike decides, or someone else?"

If they say "Mike":
> "Got it. What's the best time to catch him for two minutes? If he says no, I'll leave you guys alone."

If they say no again: thank them, log it, and try the owner at a different time or channel (Section 7). If they say "don't call us," that's a do-not-call request: **add the number to the internal DNC list the same day** (SETTER_SCRIPT rule 8).

### 6. "We already have someone for that." / "We already have a website."
> "Oh nice, who handles it for you?"
*(Listen.)*
> "Makes sense. We noticed [specific gap: it's not loading well on phones / there's no online booking / it's just the Facebook page], so we built a version that fixes that. It's just a demo. Mike can tell me in 15 minutes whether it's any better. When's a good time to catch him?"

If "someone" is the owner's nephew or a friend: never knock them. "That's great. This is just something to compare against."

### 7. "Can I take a message?"
> "Yes, please, thank you. It's Jordan with Vanguard. The short version: we built a free demo website for [Business Name] and I'd like to walk Mike through it, about 15 minutes. My number's [number]."

Then:
> "And so I'm not playing phone tag, is there a time today or tomorrow I should try him back?"

Always take the message *and* get a callback time. See Section 7 for the exact message.

### 8. "Who are you with?" / "What company?"
> "Vanguard. We're a small web design company that works with local businesses like [salons / contractors / auto shops]. We built a demo website for [Business Name]. Is Mike around?"

Answer instantly and plainly. Hesitating on this question is the #1 thing that makes you sound like a scammer. You can add relevant social proof from SETTER_SCRIPT ("We've worked with salons like Elixa Beauty") only if it's true and approved.

### 9. "He doesn't take sales calls."
> "That's fair, a lot of owners don't, and honestly I'd probably screen this too. It's not a typical pitch: we already built the demo, it's done. Is there a way he *does* like to see stuff like this? Email, text, a time he's at the desk?"

If they still say no:
> "Understood. Could I leave my name and number with you? If he's curious, he can call me. If not, no harm done."

Respect it. Don't call back three times that day. Try email to the owner and one more call at a different time later in the week.

### 10. "Just tell me and I'll pass it on."
> "I appreciate that, thank you. Here's the honest version: we built a demo website for [Business Name]. It's built around your services and your reviews. I'd want Mike to see it on a quick call so he can ask questions. Could you tell him Jordan from Vanguard called about that, and let me know when's a good time to reach him?"

If they ask "What's the website?" or "Send it to me": 
> "I'd rather walk Mike through it himself so he gets the full picture. It's quick. What's the best time to catch him?"

Why: per `DEMO_PLAYBOOK.md`, a demo that gets "passed along" loses its context. The owner sees a random link with no explanation.

**Exception: co-owners and spouses who run the business together.** If the person says "I'm his wife, we own it together" or "I make those decisions," they *are* a decision-maker. Switch to the owner opener in SETTER_SCRIPT and ask: "Do you two usually decide on things like this together? Great, let's find a time you can both see it."

## 5. Getting the owner's name, best time, and direct number (honestly)

### Getting the owner's name
Best: find it before you dial. Check the Google Business Profile ("From the owner" posts, review replies signed by name), the Facebook "About" or posts, Instagram bio, Yelp, the state contractor license lookup for trades, or the Secretary of State business filing. Log it in the CRM.

If you couldn't find it, ask:
> "I'm hoping you can help me out. Who's the owner there?"
> "And how do you say his last name, just so I get it right?" *(optional, shows care)*

If they won't say:
> "No problem. Is the owner usually the one who handles things like the website and marketing?"

### Asking when they're usually in
> "When's the best time to catch him for two minutes? First thing in the morning, or after the shop closes?"

Offer two specific options. "When is he in?" gets "It varies." "Mornings or after 5?" gets an answer.

Jeb Blount's *Fanatical Prospecting* (per reader notes and summaries) suggests calling **early or late**, because the boss is often in before the gatekeeper arrives and stays after they leave. At local businesses that's even more true: owners open the shop and close it. Stay inside **8am to 9pm in the prospect's time zone** (our compliance rule).

### The callback-time commitment
Don't leave a call with "try back later." Leave with a time:
> "Perfect. I'll call back at 7:30 tomorrow morning. Is that the best number, or does he have a line he picks up when he's out on jobs?"

Then:
- Put the exact time in your calendar and the CRM.
- **Call at exactly that time.** Being on time is what makes the gatekeeper trust you next time ("Oh, he actually called back").
- Open the next call with Opener 8 (honest callback).

### The owner's direct number or cell
You can ask for it. You just can't trick anyone into giving it.

> "Is there a better number to catch him on when he's out on jobs?"

If they hesitate:
> "Totally understand if you'd rather not give out his cell. Could you text it to him that Jordan from Vanguard called, and give him my number? Or tell me a time he'll be at the shop?"

Rules:
- If they say no to giving the cell, accept it. Don't ask twice.
- **Never** pretend to be a customer, supplier, or someone he knows to get a cell number.
- A cell number you get still has to pass our compliance check (DNC scrub, internal DNC list, calling hours) before you dial it. Note in the CRM where the number came from ("given by front desk, Jess, 10/15").
- **Never text** that number until the owner himself says yes to texts on a call (SETTER_SCRIPT texting rule).

## 6. Niche notes

Check the listed hours first. All times are the prospect's local time and must stay within 8am to 9pm.

### Salons, barbershops, nail, lash, spas (`beauty-wellness.md`)
- **Who answers:** front desk at bigger salons, otherwise a stylist or barber who may be mid-cut. Nail salons may have a language barrier: slow down, keep it simple.
- **Best time:** the first 15 to 30 minutes after opening, before the first client, and weekday mid-afternoon lulls (roughly 2 to 4pm, Tue to Thu).
- **Avoid:** Friday afternoon, all day Saturday, lunch hour at walk-in barbershops, pre-holiday and prom or wedding rush.
- **Line:** "Sounds busy, I'll be quick. Is Tasha free between clients today? I can call back right when she's got a gap."
- **Watch for:** booth-rent shops. The barber who answers may not be the owner. Ask: "Are you the owner, or do you rent a chair there?" A renter can't buy for the shop (red flag in `beauty-wellness.md`).
- Massage therapists often have their phone off during sessions. Expect voicemail.

### Trades: roofers, contractors, HVAC, plumbing, landscaping (`trades.md`)
- **Who answers:** spouse, office manager, or an answering service. The owner is on site.
- **Best time:** early morning before crews roll out (from 8am), around lunch, and early evening after 5 when they're off site. Mid-morning and afternoon they're on a roof.
- **Avoid:** right after storms for roofers (they're slammed), and early-morning emergency hours for plumbers.
- **Treat the spouse or office manager as an influencer.** They often run the books and the calendar. Be extra respectful and give them the honest one-liner so they can pass it on well.
- **Line:** "I figured he's out on a job. Is he easier to catch first thing in the morning or after 5?"
- **Line for the office manager:** "Do you handle his schedule? Could I get 15 minutes on his calendar one evening this week to show him the demo?"

### Restaurants and food (`pet-and-local-services.md`)
- **Who answers:** host, server, or a manager in the middle of service.
- **Best time:** between lunch and dinner, roughly 2 to 4pm. Food trucks: before they open.
- **Never call during service** (roughly 11:30am to 1:30pm and 5 to 8pm). If you accidentally do: "Sounds like you're in the middle of it. When's a quiet time to reach the owner?" and hang up fast.
- **Line:** "Hi, Jordan with Vanguard. Is the owner in this afternoon? I'll keep it to two minutes."

### Gyms, CrossFit, martial arts, yoga (`fitness.md`)
- **Who answers:** front desk during peak hours, a coach, or the owner.
- **Best time:** late morning to early afternoon (roughly 10am to 2pm) between class blocks. Martial arts schools often open mid-afternoon; call just before kids' classes or late morning.
- **Avoid:** 5 to 8am and 4 to 8pm (peak classes), Saturday morning classes.
- **Use their title:** "Is Coach Ramirez around?" or "Is Professor Silva in?" if that's what the school uses.
- **Line:** "When's Coach usually between classes? I'll call right then."

### Auto repair, body shops, towing, detailing (`auto.md`)
- **Who answers:** service writer or front counter. Owner is in the bays.
- **Best time:** right at opening (before the bays fill), the mid-afternoon lull, and near closing.
- **Avoid:** the morning drop-off rush and lunchtime pickups.
- **Line:** "Is Dave off the lift at all today? I just need two minutes when he's got a breather."
- **Towing:** the owner or dispatcher may answer any time but will cut you off for a call. Offer a callback: "Sounds like you might get a call any second. When's a slow time I can try you back?"

### Pet groomers, cleaners, daycares (`pet-and-local-services.md`)
- **Groomers:** owner is usually grooming. Call at opening or late afternoon.
- **Cleaners:** owner is on jobs. Call early morning or early evening.
- **Daycares:** avoid drop-off and pickup. Early-afternoon nap time is best. Ask for the director or owner.
- **Line (daycare):** "I know mornings and pickups are crazy. Is nap time a good time to catch the director?"

## 7. Leaving a message, and when to move on

### When to leave a message with the gatekeeper
Leave one when:
- They offer to take one, **and** you've also got (or tried for) a callback time.
- You've reached the same person twice and they're clearly not going to transfer you.
- It's your 2nd or 3rd attempt and you want the owner to hear "Vanguard" before your next call.

Don't leave one on the very first attempt if you haven't asked for a good time yet. A message alone puts you in a pile of callback slips.

### What to say (word for word)
> "Could you please tell Mike that Jordan from Vanguard called? We built a free demo website for [Business Name] and I'd like to walk him through it, it takes about 15 minutes. My number is [number]. And I'll try him again [day] at [time] like you suggested. Thank you so much, Jess."

Rules:
- Your name, Vanguard, the honest reason in one sentence, your number, when you'll call back.
- **Don't** say "urgent," "important," "about his account," or "he'll know what it's about."
- **Don't** give the demo link to pass along (DEMO_PLAYBOOK).
- Thank them by name.

### Voicemail (if transferred to the owner's voicemail)
Use the SETTER_SCRIPT voicemail, under 25 seconds:
> "Hey [First Name], this is [Your Name] from Vanguard. We actually built a demo redesign of your website for [Business Name] and wanted to walk you through it, it takes about 15 minutes. Give me a call back at [number] or I'll try you again in a couple days. Talk soon."

Then send the same-day "Demo site for [Business Name]" email from SETTER_SCRIPT.

### When to move on
- **On a single call:** two pushbacks, then get a time or leave a message and end politely. Don't argue a third time.
- **Across calls:** Apollo's gatekeeper guide notes returns drop off after three to five attempts. Our working rule: up to **4 phone attempts** on a lead, each at a **different time window** (opening, midday, late afternoon, near close), then switch to email and DM-only follow-up (SETTER_SCRIPT templates).
- **Switch channels when the phone is locked down.** Blount points out that few people let their gatekeeper into their social inboxes. For local owners, an Instagram or Facebook DM to the business page often reaches the owner directly. Use the SETTER_SCRIPT DM templates.
- **Stop immediately** if anyone says "don't call us," "take us off your list," or similar. Add the number to the internal do-not-call list the same day.
- **Disqualify** if you learn the business is closing, for sale, or the person answering is a booth renter with no say over the shop.

### What to log in the CRM after every gatekeeper call
- Gatekeeper's name and role ("Jess, front desk")
- Owner's name (and pronunciation if tricky)
- Best time they gave, and the exact callback time you committed to
- Any number they gave and where it came from
- What they said (smokescreen used)
- DNC request, if any

## 8. Practice drills (15 minutes a day for your first week)

1. **Speed check.** Record yourself saying Opener 1. If it's under 3 seconds, you're rushing. Aim for a relaxed 4 to 5 seconds.
2. **Rapid-fire smokescreens.** A partner reads the 10 smokescreens in random order. You answer each in one breath, ledge first.
3. **Two-pushback drill.** Partner pushes back twice. Practice ending gracefully with a time and a message, not a fight.
4. **The honesty test.** For any line you write yourself, ask: "If the owner heard this word for word, would he feel misled?" If yes, rewrite it.

## 9. Cheat sheet (one page)

**Rule zero:** Name + Vanguard in the first sentence. Never lie about who you are, why you're calling, or whether he's expecting you.

**Tone:** slow down, downward inflection, short, polite, "please" and "thank you." Ask for help, not access.

**Openers**
- "Hi, this is Jordan with Vanguard. Is Mike around?"
- "Hi, this is Jordan with Vanguard. Could you please let Mike know I'm on the line, please?"
- "Hi, I'm hoping you can help me out. Jordan with Vanguard. Who's the owner there?"
- "I'll be straight with you, this is a sales call. We already built a demo website for [Business]. Is Mike in today?"
- "Sounds busy, I'll be quick. Is Tasha free between clients today?"

**Smokescreens: ledge, honest answer, small ask**

|---|---|
| What's this regarding? | "We built a free demo website for [Business] and I want to show it to Mike. Is he in?" |
| Is he expecting you? | "No, this is a cold call, I'll be honest. Is there a good time to catch him?" |
| He's busy / with a client | "No problem. Between clients, or after he's off the job site?" |
| Send an email | "Happy to. What's his direct email? And when should I follow up by phone?" |

| He doesn't take sales calls | "Fair. It's already built. How does he like to see stuff like this?" |
| Tell me, I'll pass it on | "Thank you. I'd rather walk him through it himself. When's a good time to reach him?" |

**Always leave with:** owner's name, gatekeeper's name, a specific callback time (and call exactly then).

**Best times (their local time, 8am to 9pm only)**
- Salon/barber: just after opening, 2 to 4pm Tue to Thu. Never Saturday.
- Trades/cleaners: early morning, lunch, after 5.
- Restaurants: 2 to 4pm. Never during service.
- Gyms: 10am to 2pm. Never 5 to 8am or 4 to 8pm.
- Auto: at opening, mid-afternoon, near close.
- Daycare: nap time (early afternoon).

**Move on:** 2 pushbacks per call, about 4 attempts per lead at different times, then email and DM. "Don't call" goes on the DNC list today.

**Never:** "returning his call," "he's expecting me," "it's personal," "about your Google listing," pretend to be a customer, send the demo link to the gatekeeper, text a number without the owner's own yes.
